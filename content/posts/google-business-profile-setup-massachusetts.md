# How to Set Up a Google Business Profile That Actually Ranks

A Google Business Profile is free, takes about an hour to set up properly, and decides whether anyone within ten miles of you ever finds your business. For most Massachusetts trades, salons and shops it does more work than the website does. That is not an argument against websites. It is an argument for doing this first.

I set these up for clients alongside their sites, and the difference between a profile that ranks and one that does not is almost never effort. It is a handful of specific choices, made early, that are painful to reverse later. Here is how to make them correctly.

## What Google says it is measuring

Google publishes its own answer, and it is worth reading before you touch anything. Local ranking comes down to three things: relevance, distance and prominence ([Google Business Profile Help, "Improve your local ranking on Google"](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091?hl=en)).

**Relevance** is how well your profile matches what somebody typed. **Distance** is how far you are from them, which you cannot change. **Prominence** is how well known your business is — review count, review scores, and links to your site from elsewhere on the web.

Google also states plainly that there is no way to pay for a better local ranking. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

That leaves you two levers: make the profile unmistakably relevant, and build prominence over time. Everything below serves one of those two.

## Step one: claim it before you build it

Search your business name plus your town. There is a reasonable chance a profile already exists — Google generates them from other data sources, and old ones linger from previous owners. Claiming an existing profile keeps whatever review history is attached to it. Creating a duplicate splits your history and eventually gets one of them suspended.

Verification usually happens by postcard, phone, email or video, and Google decides which. Video verification has become common for service-area businesses and it means recording your van, your tools, your signage and your work in one continuous take. Have that ready before you start rather than scrambling on the day.

## Step two: pick the primary category carefully

This is the single most consequential field on the profile, and it is the one most people rush.

Your **primary category** does most of the work in matching you to searches. Secondary categories help, but they carry far less weight. Choose the category that describes what you do, not what you aspire to do.

A few Massachusetts examples:

- A mobile detailer should be **Car detailing service**, not **Car wash**. Different search intent entirely.
- A barber cutting men's hair should be **Barber shop**, not **Hair salon**, even though both are technically true.
- A landscaper who mostly plows in winter should still lead with **Landscaper** and add **Snow removal service** as secondary.
- An electrician doing residential work should be **Electrician**, not **Contractor**.

Test it yourself. Search the term a customer would use, look at who appears in the map results, and check what category those businesses are using. If the top three all use one category and you have chosen another, you have your answer.

## Step three: address or service area, not both

Google treats these as different kinds of business, and getting it wrong is the most common cause of suspension.

If customers come to you — a salon, a shop, a garage — show your address. If you go to them — detailing, plumbing, landscaping, mobile services — hide the address and set a service area instead. Do not display a home address for a business customers never visit. Google's guidelines are explicit that your listed location has to be somewhere you are actually staffed during stated hours ([Google Business Profile Help, "Guidelines for representing your business on Google"](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en)).

Keep the service area realistic. A detailer in Foxboro listing Foxborough, Mansfield, Walpole, Norton, Wrentham, Plainville and Sharon is credible. The same detailer listing all of Massachusetts is not, and the wider area does not help you rank further away — distance is measured from your actual location regardless.

## Step four: fill in every field, including the ones that seem pointless

Relevance is largely a function of completeness. Work through all of it:

- **Business name** exactly as it appears on your signage. Do not add keywords. "Clover Downs Detailing" is correct. "Clover Downs Detailing — Best Mobile Car Detailing Beverly MA" is a suspension waiting to happen.
- **Hours**, including holiday hours. Google surfaces "open now" heavily.
- **Services**, listed individually with short descriptions. Most businesses leave this empty. It is free relevance.
- **Attributes** — appointments required, wheelchair accessible entrance, women-owned, whatever applies.
- **Description**, 750 characters, written for a person rather than stuffed with town names.
- **Website link**, pointing at the most relevant page rather than always the homepage.
- **Opening date**, which is a small trust signal.

## Step five: photographs, and the reason yours matter more than you think

Photos are where most profiles are weakest and where the gap is easiest to close.

Upload your own. Your shop, your van, your team, your finished work. Not stock images, not manufacturer product shots. Ten good photos of real work beat forty generic ones, and a profile with no photos reads as abandoned.

Add a few every month. A profile that is being maintained looks different from one that was filled in once in 2023 and left, and customers can tell even if they could not explain how.

## Step six: reviews, asked for properly

Review count and score feed directly into prominence. Google says so.

Ask at the moment the customer is happiest, which is when you hand the work back, not three weeks later by email. Send them a direct review link rather than instructions. Respond to every review, including the bad ones, in a couple of sentences without arguing.

What you must not do: offer discounts or anything else in exchange for reviews, post reviews of your own business, or ask a friend who was never a customer. Google's guidelines prohibit incentivised reviews, and review filtering catches bursts of them.

Steady beats fast. Two genuine reviews a month for a year is a much better position than twenty in one week.

## Google Business Profile mistakes that get a listing suspended

Almost every suspension I have seen traces to one of these:

1. Keywords stuffed into the business name.
2. A virtual office, mailbox or coworking address used as a storefront.
3. A service-area business displaying a home address.
4. Duplicate profiles for the same location.
5. A business category that does not match what the business visibly does.
6. Fake or incentivised reviews.

Reinstatement is possible but slow, and you argue it with a form rather than a person. Not triggering it is enormously easier.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does it take for a Google Business Profile to rank?**
Verification takes days to a couple of weeks. Meaningful movement in the map results usually takes one to three months of a complete profile with steady reviews and photos. There is no way to accelerate it with payment.

**Do I need a website to have a Google Business Profile?**
No. You can run a profile without one. But the website link is one of the few prominence signals you directly control, and a profile pointing at a real site converts far better than one pointing nowhere. [INTERNAL LINK: /posts/facebook-page-vs-website-small-business "More on whether you need a site at all"]

**Should I list my home address if I work out of my house?**
No. Set a service area and hide the address. Displaying a home address you do not staff for customers is against Google's guidelines and is a common cause of suspension.

**How many categories should I choose?**
One primary category chosen carefully, then only the secondary categories that genuinely describe services you offer. Adding unrelated categories dilutes relevance rather than broadening it.

**Can I set up a Google Business Profile myself?**
Yes, entirely. It costs nothing and takes an hour or two if you have your photos and details ready. The reason people pay for it is the category and service-area decisions, and the reinstatement process if something goes wrong.
